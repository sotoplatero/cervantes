# Writing craft — how the draft gets written so it grabs

Cervantes' toolbox for writing a complete draft with rhythm, story, and a hook — without inventing anything and without AI noise. Applied in the author's language, filtered through `voice/profile.md`.

This file governs the **prose**. The **shape** of an issue — length tiers, the outline and its brief,
word budgets per section, writing section by section, and the seams pass — lives in `STRUCTURE.md`, and
it runs *before* any of the craft below gets applied.

## 1. One idea

Each issue defends ONE thing. If the material holds more, save it for another issue. Single focus is what gets it read end to end.

## 2. The hook (the first 2 sentences)

The reader decides in 3 seconds. Open with the most concrete thing available:
- a **scene** or **real moment** (better than a generic question),
- a **fact that jars**, or
- a **tension/contradiction** ("I thought X; the opposite happened").

Avoid the marketing-template hook ("What if I told you…?") — that's AI noise. In email, the first line also seeds the **preview text**, so it must earn the open on its own.

**Method:** `HOOK-FORMULAS.md` § Method. The tension is always one of five — curiosity, pain, contradiction, desire, identification — the same list the brief records (`STRUCTURE.md` §9). The formula gives the angle; it never overrides the golden rule or the voice.

## 3. Story, not a list

People remember stories and connect with people, not bullets. Minimum structure: **before → what happened → after**. There's a change, and it shows. Bullets only when they genuinely help scanning, never to narrate. Whose story it is matters — see §13.

The failure this section exists to prevent has a recognizable shape: a draft that is correct, sourced and
free of AI tics, and that nobody would finish, because it's a stack of findings with subheads on top. The
research gets laid out one link after another, every section restarts, and the author's point shows up
once in the middle and never again. Every item below is a guard against that.

### The spine comes before the outline

Before any outline exists, write the piece as a story **in five or six plain sentences**, the way the
author would tell it to a friend across a table. It goes in the brief (`STRUCTURE.md` §9) and it is what
the outline is cut from. If it can't be told that way, there's no story yet — and no amount of good
sections will produce one.

The beats depend on the **type of piece** the brief records:

| Type | Spine beats, in order |
|---|---|
| argument | root → problem → what nobody says (only if the material holds it) → turn → takeaway → landing |
| story | the scene → what was at stake → what happened, in order → the moment it turned → where it stands now |
| how-to | the real problem → what was tried, failures included → the steps that worked → the traps → the reader's first step |
| announcement | what it is, concretely → who it's for and what it solves → how it came about → what to do now |
| reflective essay | the observation → where it comes from in the author's life → what it complicates → where the author has landed, for now |

The argument beats, which the other types borrow from, in detail:

1. **The root.** The concrete moment that made the author write this: the note they wrote, the thing that
   broke, the number they saw, the conversation, the frustration. It's the first scene of the piece or very
   close to it — never parked at the end as an afterthought. If the author's material doesn't say what
   started it, that is the first interview question.
2. **The problem, told critically.** Who has it, what it costs them, why the usual answers fail. The
   reader has to feel the problem before any solution is worth reading. Look at it from more than one side;
   a problem described in one flat line gives the solution nothing to push against.
3. **The thing nobody says.** The uncomfortable or unexpected truth inside the topic — the motive people
   don't admit, the cost nobody counts, the reason the obvious advice doesn't work. This is usually what
   makes the piece worth forwarding, and it almost always comes from the author, not from the research.
   If you can't find it in the material, ask for it; if there isn't one, drop the beat. A reveal
   manufactured to fill the slot is the `[flat]` macro generalization in `ANTI-SLOP.md`.
4. **The turn.** What the author did, found or changed — the solution, the decision, the discovery.
5. **What the reader takes away.** Something they can do, starting today: a first step, a method, a
   decision rule, an example close enough to their own life that they can copy it. "Interesting" is not a
   takeaway.
6. **The landing.** The last concrete thing. A return to the root (the callback, §5) and the central idea
   said once more, plainly, **when the material offers them** — never bolted on as a moral.

### The author's experience is the thread; research is support

The spine is the author's story. Research enters **only where it moves that story forward**, and it enters
as part of the author's argument, in their voice — "you can't expect to make money from AI just by
learning to write prompts" — not as a report on what a source said. Don't narrate the research process,
don't name the tools that found things, don't string findings together with links. A finding that doesn't
serve the spine stays in the `researcher` brief, however good it is. How many sources go in is never a
number; it's however many the story needs.

A **minor signal gets a sentence, not a section.** Section-level space goes to the beats of the spine.

### Every seam is "but" or "so", never "and then"

Read the outline as a chain: each section should follow from the previous one by **but** (a complication,
an objection, the thing that went wrong) or **so** (a consequence, the next step it forces). If the link
between two sections is only "and also" or "and then", they are items on a list, not parts of a story — and
the reader feels the jump even when they can't name it. Find the real link, reorder, or merge.

This is the same continuity `STRUCTURE.md` §6 asks for, seen from the story's side: the subhead is a pause,
the "but" or "so" is what carries the reader across it.

### The central idea survives every restructure

The single idea (§1) is stated near the top and carried by every section. When a
draft gets reorganized — new sections, numbered steps, a split into free and paid — check afterwards that
the idea is still there, said plainly, and not reduced to a line inside a subsection. Restructures are
where theses get lost.

### The type of piece is a decision, not a drift

An argument, a story, a how-to and a tool announcement are different pieces with different spines. The type
is chosen in the brief, and it holds. When a request mid-draft would turn one into another (strip the
argument and keep only the steps; turn the story into a checklist), name the change to the author before
making it — a piece that switches type halfway loses the reason anyone was reading. When a how-to does
belong in the piece, it comes **after** the problem and the turn, never instead of them: the steps only
matter to a reader who already wants the result.

### Plain words carry the story

Say the thing directly. A metaphor or an image has to be the author's or has to make something clearer
than the plain sentence would; decorative ones and wise-sounding abstractions are `[flat]` in
`ANTI-SLOP.md`. When in doubt, write the plain version.

## 4. Open loops

Open a question or tension early and **pay it off** later (Zeigarnik effect). Two levels:
- **Macro:** one tension running through the issue, resolved at the end.
- **Micro:** small paragraph-to-paragraph questions that close fast and pull to the next line.

Rule of honor: **every loop opened gets closed.** Otherwise it's clickbait.

## 5. The callback

A detail, phrase, or image from the opening that reappears at the end — **when the material offers one**. Closes the circle; small reward for the reader. A callback forced in reads as a device.

## 6. Rhythm and cadence

The music of prose — measured against **this author**, not against a recipe:
- **Sentence length follows the profile's Sentence rhythm row.** What matters is variance: a run of sentences of the same length reads as machine cadence, whatever that length is. No fixed pattern (short-short-long becomes its own tic).
- **Vary sentence openings.** Not all subject-verb-object; sometimes open with a subordinate clause or a concrete detail.
- **Paragraph size follows the profile's Paragraph size row**, varied around it. A long paragraph is fine when it develops one point; a one-line paragraph lands one, and spent on every point it becomes the dramatic pause `ANTI-SLOP.md` tags `[freq]`.
- **Read-aloud test.** If you run out of breath or it sounds choppy, something's off. The sentence that sounds natural spoken is the author's voice.
- **Carry the thread across subheads.** Rhythm isn't only inside the paragraph. A heading is a visual pause, never a fresh start: the section under it continues the thought the previous one left standing. `STRUCTURE.md` §6 and the seams pass in §7 govern this.

## 7. Concrete > abstract (show, don't tell)

A real detail outweighs a declared emotion. Not "I felt vertigo" — the concrete fact that produces it ("it was the third time that week"). The fact does the work — and nothing gets invented that the author didn't give.

## 8. Subheads that guide (required)

Every issue has a **title**, a **dek** (the promise under the title), and **internal subheads**, each concrete and in the author's voice, so a skim of the subheads alone gives the shape of the issue. How many, and when an author's letter-style archive switches them off, is `STRUCTURE.md` §5 — the one density rule. In a long issue the subheads are the outline rows agreed in the brief. No "The Definitive Guide: Everything You Need to Know" or colon formats (`ANTI-SLOP.md` § The colon gloss).

## 9. Plain words, zero filler

Short sentence over long if they say the same thing. Cut adverbs and inflated adjectives ("powerful," "robust," "incredible"). The closing is **earned** by what was told; no free universal moral.

## 10. Subject, preheader & CTA (the part that converts)

Written LAST, from the finished draft, when the most compelling hook is known. The rules live only in `TEMPLATES.md` § Subject line, § Preheader, § CTA.

## 11. Research and cite (situate only where it serves the spine)

Outside context enters **only where it moves a beat of the spine** (§3): what the thing is really called, who did it before, the debate, a number — each one when the author's argument needs it, as a sentence in their voice, with a **real, checked link**. Context that serves no beat stays in the `researcher` brief. There is no "context" section to fill.

**Links go INLINE, on the word where the claim lives.** This is a newsletter, **not a thesis**: NO references list, bibliography, "Sources," or footnotes at the end. Never invent what a source says; cite in passing, without copying paragraphs.

**And links are rationed** — one rule, by tier: `short` 0–2, `standard` 2–4, `deep` 3–5, never more than one per section, and only where the claim genuinely rests on the source (a number, a name, prior art, something a reader might not take on trust). Linking a term the reader already knows, or stacking three in a paragraph, turns the page into a link farm and costs the reader the thread at every one of them. When a source is worth keeping but the sentence doesn't need it, leave it out: it stays in the `researcher` brief, and a `[[note: ...]]` can offer it to the author without spending the reader's attention.

## 12. Guiding notes (placeholders with meaning)

Alongside the text, leave deletable notes for the author:
- `[[note: ...]]` — an observation or heads-up.
- `[[idea: ...]]` — a suggestion they can take or leave.
- `[[fact?: ...]]` — something they may want to add or verify.
- `[[enrich: ...]]` — points to where the text lands harder with something only the author has: a concrete example, a personal anecdote, their reaction, a real number. Mark the opening; never fill it with invented material.

Seed `[[enrich: ...]]` deliberately where the author's own detail beats any outside context. **If they delete every note, the text still works.**

## 13. Write to ONE reader — who is the hero

Not "hey friends" — from the reader's side there's no room full of other subscribers; it's just them, addressed the way the profile records (tú / usted / vosotros / you), the same way throughout. The letter (voice, point of view) matters more than the news.

And in that letter, **the reader is the hero; the author is the guide** (StoryBrand). The reader has the problem, the gap, the transformation to live; the author points the way — they don't stand center stage taking a bow. Practically, the reader should finish the issue seeing *themselves* changed, not just admiring the author.

This does NOT cancel a confessional, first-person voice. When the author tells their own story — their failure, their number, their mistake — it works precisely because it's the reader's **mirror**: "the same thing is about to bite you." Author's story = the vehicle; reader's transformation = the destination. Tell yours so they see theirs.

**Anti-formula guard:** don't stamp a hero's-journey mold on every issue (personage → gap → guide → transformation → result, week after week). Applied mechanically it becomes the exact template-slop `ANTI-SLOP.md` bans — and it makes every issue sound identical. Use the arc when the material invites it; skip it when a plain observation or a single scene lands harder. The frame serves the story, never the reverse.

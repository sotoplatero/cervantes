# Anti-slop — banned patterns (always enforced)

"AI noise" is the single biggest tell that a machine wrote the text. Cervantes never produces the patterns below — in drafts, polish, titles, subjects, subheads, or cover copy.

## The exception rule (read first)

A pattern is allowed ONLY if `cervantes/profile.md` documents it as a legitimate signature of THIS author's real voice (observed in their published text, not assumed) — and even then only when it comes from a real tension, never as empty filler. A documented exception in the profile overrides this list.

## The frequency rule (read second)

Several devices below are legitimate craft **once**, and a tell the moment they become a habit: the one-word dramatic pause, the trailing ellipsis, the rule of three, the aphorism, the mid-sentence dash. They got overused because they worked. So the test is density, not existence:

> **Once per issue, earned by real tension: fine. Twice: cut one. A per-section reflex: it's a tic, and readers who see AI text all day will clock it.**

Everything under *Rhythm tics* is governed by this rule. Everything else is a flat ban.

## Banned patterns

### Formulaic openings
- The "something made me think" preamble: *"Hace unos días leí algo que me hizo pensar…"*, *"Recientemente me topé con…"*. Open with the thought itself.
- Scene-setting from nowhere: *"En un mundo donde…"*, "in the fast-paced world of," "imagine for a moment." Nobody starts a real conversation this way.
- Rhetorical questions as the first line: *"¿Y si nos pusiéramos de acuerdo en…?"*, "What if I told you…?," "Have you ever…?" The hypothetical + inclusive "we" is a machine favorite: it manufactures community with no one in the room.

### Antithesis and manufactured contrast
- *"No es X. Es Y."* / "It's not X, it's Y" / "From X to Y" / "X? No. Y." / "not only X, but Y" / "Stop X, start Y."
- The reframed-question move: *"La pregunta no es si vas a usar X. La pregunta es cómo."*
- Allowed only when the contrast genuinely stings — when Y actually contradicts X and the author can show it. Decorative contrast is banned.

### Announced effects
The text claims an effect instead of producing it. Always cut the announcement; keep whatever follows, if anything is really there.
- Announced honesty: *"Permíteme ser honesto contigo"*, *"Te voy a ser sincero"*, "to be honest with you." Real honesty doesn't ask permission — it just shows up.
- Announced depth: *"Esto me hizo reflexionar profundamente"*, *"Hay algo más profundo aquí"*. Depth is visible in the next paragraph or it isn't there.
- Announced importance: "and that changed everything," "spoiler:," "this is the key."

### Filler bridges and empty profundity
- Connective tissue that joins two ideas that don't connect: *"Porque hay algo más profundo en juego"*, *"Y ahí está la clave"*, "the reality is that." If the bridge is load-bearing, the ideas don't belong in the same paragraph.
- The too-perfect aphorism — a sentence so balanced it reads as generated: *"La ventaja competitiva no está en tener la herramienta. Está en saber cuándo no usarla."* Real insight arrives lopsided and specific. If it would fit any newsletter in the niche, cut it.
- Macro generalization with nothing under it: *"cuando todos seguimos la misma recomendación, el mercado deja de diferenciar."* Sweeping claims need the author's data, the author's experience, or a real cited source — otherwise state it small and concrete.
- Universal-lesson closing the text didn't earn.

### Rhythm tics (frequency rule applies)
- Empty **tricolon**: "fast, simple, and powerful." And the anaphoric trio — three short parallel sentences in a row.
- **Lists of three** as a default shape. Reality rarely comes in threes; use the number of items there actually are.
- The **one-word dramatic pause** on its own line. ("Silencio." / "Nada.")
- The **trailing ellipsis** used to manufacture suspense…
- **Mid-sentence dash density.** The dash is legitimate punctuation — in Spanish the *raya* has always marked incisos — but AI reaches for it where a comma, a colon, or a full stop belongs. Cap it at roughly one per section, and never as a substitute for deciding how two clauses relate.
- Symmetric sentence pairs used as decoration rather than meaning.

### Inflated and dead vocabulary
- Adjectives with no proof: *"transformador"*, *"disruptivo"*, "powerful," "robust," "fascinating," "revolutionary," "incredible," "seamless," "game-changer." When everything transforms, nothing does.
- Exhausted metaphors, above all *"navegar"* (la incertidumbre, el cambio, la complejidad). Name the actual action: choosing, postponing, cutting, paying.
- **Colon subheads**: "Title: the definitive guide."

### Ritual engagement
- The reflex closing question: *"¿Tú cómo lo ves?"*, *"¿Y tú qué opinas?"* Asking is fine; asking the way everyone asks is wallpaper. Ask something only this issue could ask, or close on a statement.
- The altruistic share CTA: *"Comparte si crees que esto puede ayudar a alguien."* It's engineered to lower resistance, and the engineering shows.

## Language

Equivalents in the author's language count too — the list is about the *pattern*, not the wording. The Spanish examples are quoted verbatim because surface forms are what's actually checkable; when writing in another language, ban that language's equivalents.

## The test

If a sentence could be signed by anyone, it isn't the author's. Rewrite it from a concrete detail in their material, or cut it.
